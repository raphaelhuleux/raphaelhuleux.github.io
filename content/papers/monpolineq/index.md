##### Abstract

We study how the distribution of permanent labor income (PLI) affects the transmission of fiscal policy. Standard heterogeneous-agent models have two sharp implications: (i) the distribution of PLI is neutral for aggregate variables, and (ii) the share of hand-to-mouth households does not vary with PLI. Using US household survey data, we show instead that the hand-to-mouth share falls from 64% in the bottom PLI quintile to 22% in the top one. We then build a heterogeneous-agent New Keynesian (HANK) model with a non-homothetic bequest motive that can reproduce this gradient and breaks the neutrality. In standard HANK models, the multiplier of a fiscal package depends mostly on how long the debt lasts. In ours, it depends at least as much on who receives the payout and who finances it, because low-PLI households spend extra income quickly while high-PLI households save most of it. In an application to a regressive fiscal package, the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, the model gives a cumulative multiplier of −0.06, against 0.57 in a standard HANK model with the same aggregate marginal propensity to consume. For the same reason, the effect of a rate cut depends on who receives the interest the government saves on its debt. In the model, the rise in PLI inequality since 1970 has made redistributions between PLI groups move output more.
